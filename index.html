A documentation log for questions about an existing wall's drainage

Published October 11, 2026

When water appears near an existing retaining wall, it is tempting to move directly from a photograph to a repair idea. A documentation log helps preserve the intermediate questions: what was observed, when it was observed, which records explain the drainage route, and who can resolve the remaining uncertainty. The log is a tool for communication, rather than a test of the wall's stability or a construction instruction.

This article is from Seattle Slope & Wall, an independent public-record research project. It was prepared with AI assistance and checked against the project's source-linked drainage and homeowner guides. It does not diagnose a property or recommend a drainage modification.

Give each observation a date and context

Keep a short entry for each observation instead of repeatedly editing one undated description. Note the date, the weather context you actually know, and what you could see from an accessible location. Distinguish a photograph taken after rain from a conclusion about where the water originated. If you do not know when a condition began, say that explicitly.

Photographs are easier to compare when their viewpoints and framing are consistent. Retain the original images and describe the viewpoint in ordinary language. Do not treat changes in lighting, camera angle or surface wetness as measured structural movement. If measurements or access would be needed to answer a question, leave that work to the appropriate professional rather than entering an unsupported number.

Keep facts and interpretations in different columns

A useful log can have six fields: observation date, visible observation, document or photograph reference, possible explanation, unanswered question, and the person or organization that can address it. The possible-explanation field is optional. It should never be presented as a confirmed cause just because it has been repeated in several entries.

For example, “wet area visible below the wall after rain” belongs in the observation field. “Unknown connection to an existing drainage outlet” belongs in the question field. A proposed diagnosis should identify its source and date. This structure helps a future reviewer separate the owner's observations from statements made by a contractor, inspector or engineer.

Create a separate map of the known documents

List the drawings, permits, inspection records, side-sewer information, previous reports and maintenance records you already have. For each item, note the date, the portion of the property or work it covers, and whether it describes an existing system or a proposed one. A drawing for one phase of work should not silently become an as-built description of every later change.

Seattle's official starting points are the project-research page at https://www.seattle.gov/construction-and-inspections/resources/research-a-project and Seattle Public Utilities' side-sewer permit information at https://www.seattle.gov/utilities/construction-resources/sewer-and-drainage/side-sewer-permits. These sources help locate records and identify the relevant authority; they do not by themselves establish every concealed connection at a particular wall.

Preserve the unknown outlet

If the outlet has not been documented, write “outlet not established from the available records.” A visible pipe or opening can be a useful observation, but it does not show the entire route, ownership, condition, capacity or approval history. Do not replace that gap with the route that would be most convenient for a proposed repair.

Ask a prospective professional or contractor which records can clarify the route, whether investigation is needed, and who owns coordination of the proposed drainage work. Put the answer in the log with its source. If two proposals make different assumptions about the outlet, that difference belongs in the bid comparison rather than being hidden inside the total price.

Record work and changes without rewriting earlier entries

When a professional investigates the site or a contractor performs work, add a new dated entry and link the supporting document. Keep earlier entries available. A later finding may explain an earlier observation, but it should not make the log appear as though the cause was known from the beginning.

The same principle applies to public records. A permit that was Issued when you first checked it may later show Completed. Preserve both review dates and the current official source. The administrative change is useful evidence, but it does not turn the entire observation history into a professional assessment of present condition.

Use the log when requesting scope clarification

Send the same relevant packet to every respondent so their questions and proposals begin with the same information. Ask which investigation, design, drainage coordination, inspection and closeout deliverables are included. Distinguish an allowance for an unresolved condition from a fixed scope based on established information.

A short question list might ask: Which document establishes the existing route? Which part of the route remains uncertain? Who will investigate that uncertainty? What would trigger a scope change? Which records will be supplied after the work? These are organizational questions. The responsible professionals still decide what investigation and design apply to the particular site.

Keep the log usable and proportionate

An effective log does not need hundreds of photographs or a complicated application. A dated folder, a short table and a concise question list can be enough. Remove irrelevant duplicates from the working packet while retaining original records separately. Avoid including neighboring homeowners' private contact information in a packet intended for wider distribution.

If there is an immediate safety concern, documentation is secondary to obtaining appropriate assistance and following official guidance. Seattle's severe-weather resources include landslide information at https://www.seattle.gov/transportation/projects-and-programs/safety-first/severe-weather. A log is not a reason to enter a potentially unsafe area or delay professional attention.
